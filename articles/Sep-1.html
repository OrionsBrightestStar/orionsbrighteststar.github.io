2. More on Paracompactness

Let us continue our discussion of paracompactness. Now, we will begin to see the motivations behind why paracompactness is such a useful tool. Formulated as a sort of alternative to compactness, it carries some useful properties that make it possible to study manifold theory more deeply. We begin with the following definition:

Definition 1 (Support)

Suppose that $f: S \to \mathbb{R}$ is a mapping from some space $S$ to the real numbers. We say that the support of $f$ is the set $$\text{support}(f) = \text{closure}\{x \mid f(x)\not= 0\}$$

In other words, it is the set consisting of all the points in the space where $f$ is zero, or $f$ is basically "about" to be zero, in a sort of sense. As it turns out, this allows us to define a concept known as the partition of unity, which is a bunch of maps where at any point there is a neighborhood where only a finite number of maps are nonzero, and the sum of the images of the maps at each point is 1, hence the term partition of "unity."

Definition 2 (Partition of Unity)

Suppose $\{U_\alpha \mid \alpha \in A\}$ is a sort of open covering of the space $X$. Then, we define a partition of unity subordinate to this covering as a collection of maps $$\{f_\beta: X \to [0,1] \mid \beta \in B\}$$ such that the following hold:

  1. There is a locally finite open refinement $\{V_\beta \mid \beta \in B\}$ of our open covering such that $\text{support}(f_\beta)\subset V_\beta$ for all $\beta \in B$, and
  2. $\sum_\beta f_\beta (x) = 1$ for all $x\in X$.

I will omit the proof for this next theorem.

Theorem 1

If $X$ is paracompact and $\textbf{U}$ is an open covering of $X$, then there exists a partition of unity subordinate to $\textbf{U}$. Furthermore, if $\{U_\alpha\}$ is a locally finite open covering of $X$, then there is an open covering $\{V_\alpha\}$ such that, for each $\alpha$, $\overline{V}_\alpha \subset U_\alpha$.

We now consider the last definition for today, which is on $\sigma$-compactness.

Definition 3 ($\sigma$-compactness)

A space is $\sigma$-compact if it can be considered to be the union of a countable number of compact subspaces.

For example, the set of rational numbers is $\sigma$-compact. The next theorem, I will omit the proof, but the theorem after, I will supply the proof.

Theorem 2

A locally compact, Hausdorff space is paracompact if and only if it is the disjoint union of open $\sigma$-compact subsets.

Recall that a locally compact space is a topological space such that each point has a compact neighborhood (in this case, a neighborhood is not necessarily open, there just needs to be some open set that is a subset of the neighborhood with the point in the open set. Anyways, moving on,

Theorem 3

If $X$ is locally compact, Hausdorff, and second countable then its one-point compactification $X^+$ is metrizable and $X$ is $\sigma$-compact and paracompact.

Proof.

Since $X$ is second countable, then we may have a countable basis $\textbf{B}$ for $X$. Now, suppose $C \subset X$ is compact. Since $X$ is locally compact, any point $x \in C$ has a compact neighborhood $N$ aroun dit, and therefore there exists some $U_x \in \textbf{B}$ such that $x \in U_x \subset N$. Therefore, since every point is a subset of some $U_x$ in the basis, then $C$ is covered by a finite union $U_{x_1} \cup \cdots \cup U_{x_n}$, since $C$ is compact. Let $V = X - \bigcup \overline{U}_{x_i}$, which is the set of all points in $X$ not covered by the closures of one of the $U_x$ open sets. Consider the set $V \cup \{\infty\}$, which is a neighborhood of $\infty$ in the one-point compactification of $X$, $X^+$, which is contained in the neighborhood $X^+-C$. Now consider all the possible sets $C$ and all the possible choices of finite collections of basis elements. There are many sets $V$. We can index them by finite subsets of $\textbf{B}$; that is, for each finite subset of $\textbf{B}$, there is some compact set $C$ associated with it as well as a set $V$ constructed from both. Therefore, since the set of finite subsets of $\textbf{B}$ (which is countable) is countable (one can see a book on set theory to prove this fact, but it is a very trivial corollary of Cantor's Diagonalization Theorem), then all the sets $V$ are countable as well. $V$ allows us to create a local basis around the point $\infty$, so that combined with $\textbf{B}$ gives us a countable basis of $X^+$. Therefore, $X^+$ is second-countable. $X$ is apparently $\sigma$-compact as well, though I can't seem to grasp why. Using the previous theorem (the backwards direction), $X$ is paracompact. Furthermore, note that a compact Hausdorff space is normal (a previous result). A normal space is also completely regular, and finally, by the Urysohn Metrization Theorem, a space that is second countable and completely regular is metrizable. Hence, $X^+$ is metrizable.

3. A Review of Homotopy

Homotopy is a pivotal tool in algebraic topology, and we begin to see it in its most basic form. A homotopy is essentially a bunch of mappings that vary continuously, from one mapping to another. More precisely, what I mean is this:

Definition 4 (Homotopy)

Suppose $X$ and $Y$ are topological spaces. A homotopy of maps from $X$ to $Y$ is a continuous map $F: X \times [0,1] \to Y$, where we recall that $\times$ is the Cartesian product.

Furthermore, two maps $f_0,f_1:X \to Y$ are homotopic if there exists some homotopy $F:X \times [0,1] \to Y$ such that $F(x,0)=f_0(x)$ and $F(x,1)=f_1(x)$ for all $x \in X$.

Note that homotopy defines an equivalence relation, where "$f$ is homotopic to $g$" is written as $f \simeq g$.

Proposition 1

Suppose $f,g:X \to Y$, $h: X'\to X$ and $k: Y \to Y'$ are all continuous. We have that $$f \simeq g \implies f \circ h \simeq g \circ h \text{ and }k \circ f \simeq k \circ g$$

Proof.

Suppose that $f \simeq g$. Then there exists some continuous $F: X \times I \to Y$, where $I = [0,1]$, such that $F(x,0)=f(x)$ and $F(x,1)=g(x)$. Consider the map $G: X' \times I \to Y$, where $G(x',t)=F(h(x'),t)$. It is clear due to the continuity of $h$ and $F$ that $G$ is continuous as well, and that $G(x',0)=(f \circ h)(x')$ and $G(x',1)=(g \circ h)(x')$. Therefore, $f \circ h \simeq g \circ h$. A similar argument shows that $k \circ f = k \circ g$.
