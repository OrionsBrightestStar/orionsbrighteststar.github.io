2. The Implicit Function Theorem

We will skip the rest of Bredon's first chapter on point-set topology and now begin a thorough examination of smooth manifolds. We begin by examining two theorems from multivariable analysis, the implicit function theorem and the inverse function theorem.

Theorem 1 (The Mean Value Theorem)

Suppose we have a smooth function $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$. If $x=(x_1,\ldots, x_n)$ and $\overline{x}=(\overline{x}_1,\ldots, \overline{x}_n)$, then we have that $$f(x) - f(\overline{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(\tilde{x})(x_i-\overline{x}_i)$$ where $\tilde{x}$ is on the line segment between $x$ and $\overline{x}$.

Proof.

First, we consider the function $\mathbb{R}\to \mathbb{R}$ where $t \mapsto f(tx+(1-t)\overline{x})$. It necessarily follows that, by the single-variable mean value theorem, we have that there exists some $t_0$ between 0 and 1 such that $$f(1x+(1-1)\overline{x})-f(0x+(1-0)\overline{x})=f(x)-f(\overline{x})=\frac{df(tx+(1-t)\overline{x})}{dt}\bigg|_{t=t_0}$$ However, using the Chain Rule, we may find that the right hand side becomes $$\frac{df(tx+(1-t)\overline{x}}{dt}\bigg|_{t=t_0}=\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(\tilde{x}) \frac{d(tx_i+(1-t)\overline{x}_i)}{dt}\bigg|_{t=t_0}=\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(\tilde{x})(x_i-\overline{x}_i)$$ where we let $\tilde{x} = t_0x+(1-t_0)\overline{x}$. The desired expression follows shortly.

□
A corollary extending the Mean Value Theorem to functions where some of the coordinates remain fixed follows shortly.
Corollary 1

Suppose we have a smooth function $f: \mathbb{R}^k \times \mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}$ where $x\in \mathbb{R}^k$ and $y,\overline{y} \in \mathbb{R}^m$. Then we have $$f(x,y)-f(x,\overline{y})=\sum_{i=1}^m \frac{\partial f}{\partial y_i}(x,\tilde{y})(y_i-\overline{y}_i)$$ where $\tilde{y}$ is on the line segment between $y$ and $\overline{y}$.

Proof.

By the Mean Value Theorem, we have that $$f(x,y)-f(x,\overline{y})=\sum_{i=1}^k \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x,\tilde{y}) (x_i-x_i) + \sum_{i=1}^m \frac{\partial f}{\partial y_i}(x,\tilde{y})(y_i-\overline{y}_i)$$ However, note that $x_i-x_i=0$, so $$\sum_{i=1}^k \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x,\tilde{y}) (x_i-x_i)$$ must therefore be equal to zero. Hence, $$f(x,y)-f(x,\overline{y}) = \sum_{i=1}^m \frac{\partial f}{\partial y_i}(x,\tilde{y})(y_i-\overline{y}_i)$$

□

One important theorem of note is that of the Banach Contraction Principle. It essentially states that for a function that is a "contraction" (which will be defined later), there is a unique fixed point that is the limit when you apply the contraction infinitely many times to a point.

Theorem 2 (The Banach Contraction Principle)

Suppose $X$ is a complete metric space and $T: X \to X$ is a contraction, which means that for some $K<1$, $\text{dist}(Tx,Ty)\leq K \cdot \text{dist}(x,y)$ for all $x,y\in X$. It follows that $T$ has a unique fixed point $\zeta \in X$, and for any $x\in X$, $\zeta = \lim_{i \to \infty} T^i(x)$.

Proof.

Suppose we have some $x_0 \in X$, and $x_i = Tx_{i-1}$. If we have that $\delta = \text{dist}(x_0,x_1)$, then we may see by the triangle inequality that $$\text{dist}(x_0,x_k) \leq \text{dist}(x_0,x_1) + \text{dist}(x_1,x_2)+\cdots + \text{dist}(x_{k-1},x_k)$$ which, as distances are always nonnegative, implies $$ \leq \text{dist}(x_0,x_1) + \text{dist}(x_1,x_2) + \cdots + \text{dist}(x_{k-1},x_k) + \cdots$$ and still, since $\text{dist}(Tx,Ty) \leq K \cdot \text{dist}(x,y)$, we have that this implies $$\leq \text{dist}(x_0,x_1)+K \cdot \text{dist}(x_0,x_1) + K^2 \cdot \text{dist}(x_0,x_1)+\cdots$$ which, letting $\delta = \text{dist}(x_0,x_1)$, and combining terms, we obtain $$= \delta (1 + K + K^2 + \cdots)$$ and using the convergence of this specific power series as $K <1$, we obtain $$= \frac{\delta}{1-K}$$ Furthermore, when we have $m \geq n$, $$\text{dist}(x_n,x_m) \leq K \cdot \text{dist}(x_{n-1},x_{m-1})\leq K^2 \cdot \text{dist}(x_{n-2},x_{m-2})$$ $$\leq \cdots \leq K^n \cdot \text{dist}(x_0,x_{m-n}) \leq \frac{\delta K^n}{1-K}$$ As $n\to \infty$, we have that this expression goes to 0, since $K<1$. It therefore follows that $x_0,x_1,x_2,\ldots$ is a Cauchy sequence, and since $X$ is complete, then there exists a $\zeta \in X$ such that $\zeta = \lim_{i\to\infty}x_i$. It is a fixed point, as $T\zeta = \lim_{i\to\infty} T x_i = \lim_{i\to\infty}x_{i+1}=\zeta$. We now prove uniqueness. If $x$ is another fixed point, then we must have that $$\text{dist}(x,\zeta)=\text{dist}(Tx,T\zeta) \leq K \cdot \text{dist}(x,\zeta)$$. Recall that $K<1$, so this implies that the distance is zero, and therefore $x=\zeta$.
